gateau · notation study 01

Gateau Notation Sketchbook

One real data path, drawn five ways by hand, to decide what the generated diagrams should look like before writing any layout code.

subject: Parretto DisplayPort RX link layer (prt_dprx_lnk.sv) defaults: P_LANES = 4, P_SPL = 2, P_PPC = 2 status: mockups, not generated

The subject

Every stage of the RX link layer takes and returns the same record, prt_dp_rx_lnk_if: symbol data dat[ℓ][s] (8 bits each), the K-flag k[ℓ][s], six one-bit tag fields (sol eol vid sdp msa vbid) per lane and symbol, and lock. Here ℓ is the lane axis (P_LANES) and s is symbols per lane (P_SPL). Stages add tags, consume them, or rewrite the data. The RTL spells out each of these by hand: zeroing killed fields, copying unchanged ones, and re-attaching the parser's tags after the descrambler.

That makes it a good test, because the structure is real but invisible. Every drawing below shows the same design. All of them were drawn by hand from reading the RTL. Stage latencies are drawn only where the source states them (parser and scrambler, both one cycle); the others are left out rather than guessed.

candidate 1

The bus, drawn as a quantum circuit

Each field of the record gets a fixed row, as a qubit does in a circuit diagram or a belt in a Factorio main bus. A row is faint where nothing is on it and solid where a value is live. A box sits on the rows it writes; a dot connected to a box marks a row it reads, just like a control dot on a controlled gate. Rows that pass a box untouched are drawn straight through and need no code. The control plane gets the quantum convention for classical wires: a double line.

What this view shows that FIG A could not

Producers and consumers are where the rows start and stop. The fork/join stops being an invisible convention: both branches sit inside one shaded band between two register edges. Side channels (messages, SDP packets, video) leave the bus at right angles, so they never look like bus traffic. And the empty tracks to the right of MSA and SDP show the "lane freed" moment you described from Factorio.

Its weakness is that the rows are fields, so the lane axis ℓ is hidden inside the width labels. A design that rearranges lanes needs the next view.

overlay

The contract at the join

The only things keeping the join correct are two comments. prt_dprx_pars.sv:1394 says the tags have one clock of latency, and prt_dprx_scrm.sv:128 says the scrambler's input register exists "to compensate for the parser delay". There is also a commented-out block in the parser that used to delay the data instead, which suggests the contract has been rearranged at least once. A generated diagram should check this, not only draw it. Here are the two outcomes on a cycle grid, the way a pipeline diagram or a Filament timeline type would state them.

candidate 2

The axis view, in Neural Circuit Diagram style

Here each axis is a wire, not each field. This is NCD's "all broadcasted" form. It is the right view for the parts of a datapath that rearrange data rather than tag it: replicating a block per lane, picking one lane, copying a value to every lane, reducing over lanes, and regrouping lanes and symbols into pixels.

This view earns its keep on the lane-mapping blocks, which are 5,000 lines of RTL (prt_dprx_vid_lmap.sv, prt_dprx_vid_vmap.sv) whose whole job is the two bars on the right. At the top level it would double the wire count without adding any producer/consumer information, so I'd treat it as a zoom, not the default.

candidate 3

The cost view: the bus drawn to scale

Napkin reads transfer cost off its diagrams. Here the equivalent is flip-flops: if row thickness is proportional to bits, the area of the bus between two register edges is the pipeline-register cost of that stage. Drawn this way, the tag copies from FIG D become visible as area.

This one works best as a toggle on FIG B ("width ∝ bits"), the way tsncd lets you flip between forms. It doesn't need its own layout.

A glyph vocabulary

Everything used above, plus one glyph this design doesn't need yet (back-edges). The aim is a small, closed set that an algorithm can emit and a reader can learn once. Each card names where the glyph comes from.

Live row

Read

Write

Produce / consume

A row starts at a box's right edge or ends inside a box. An open dot marks a row produced through a connector.

Cycle band

Two register edges. Everything inside the band happens in one cycle, and position inside the band means nothing.

Broadcast

Clock domain

Colour is the domain, and a colour change is a CDC. Like Napkin's memory levels, a change of colour costs something.

What I took from NCD and tsncd

I read the TMLR paper end to end and rendered tsncd's published examples in a browser in all three of its display forms. Six things carry over directly:

One term, several forms. tsncd draws the same model as "arrows and boxes", "arrows and broadcasted" and "all broadcasted", switched by a toggle. That is the answer to "which of these views?": FIG A, B and D are three forms of one description, not three diagrams to maintain.

Annotations on wires. The quantised view labels each wire with its number format (FP32, INT32, BF16). The hardware equivalent is the width label, and later the clock domain.

An axis legend beside the drawing. tsncd shows a table mapping each axis symbol to its code name. Here that is ℓ → P_LANES and s → P_SPL, which keeps the drawing short.

Nested coloured sections for hierarchy, and a bracket with ×N for repetition. They map one to one onto module hierarchy and generate loops.

Cost read off the picture. In the paper, the height of a diagram tracks memory use. Napkin reads transfer cost from colour changes. FIG E is the same move.

Where it stops. The paper excludes recursive models explicitly, so there is no time and no feedback. Its free-form curved wires also stop being readable at around fifteen wires (look at the full transformer in the paper's figure 22). For hardware, fixed rows (from quantum circuits) are the discipline NCD lacks, and cycle bands are the time axis it never needed.

Where I land

The bus view should be the primary picture. On this design it answered the most questions for the least ink. The other views earn their place as overlays or zooms on it, not as separate diagrams.

primary · FIG B

Bus view

Rows are fields, boxes are stages, dots are reads, bands are cycles. This is the default rendering of a design.

overlay · FIG C, E

Contract and cost

A badge on every join (aligned or skewed), and a "width ∝ bits" toggle. These are computed from the same description, not drawn separately.

zoom · FIG D

Axis view

Open a single box to see how it rearranges ℓ, s and pixels. It should be used for lane mappers and similar blocks, never at the top level.

Decisions to make before the algorithm

  1. What the x-axis means. FIG B uses stage order with cycle bands only where latency is known. The alternative is true cycle positions, which is more precise but needs latencies for every block. I lean towards stage order, with bands wherever latency is declared.
  2. Row order. Fixed by declaration (the Factorio way: predictable, diffable) or optimised to cut connector crossings (the storyline way: prettier, but it shifts when the design changes). I lean towards fixed order with manual pins.
  3. Row granularity. One row per field (FIG B), or one per field × lane when lanes are treated differently. My view is per field, and lanes belong in the axis zoom.
  4. Control plane. Same picture as a double-line row (as drawn), or its own diagram. On your controller this depends on how much the data path and register interface interact.
  5. What your controller has that this one doesn't. The Parretto link layer is fixed-rate and feed-forward. If the work design has valid/ready backpressure, stateful memories or feedback, those cases need mocking up next, before any code. I'd draw the hardest block of it the same way, by hand, from memory.
